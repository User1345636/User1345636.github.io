Mesh reachability test — is my packet reaching the far node?

Meshvox packets are invisible in the stock Meshtastic app. That is deliberate — they travel on a private portnum so they never appear in anybody’s chat. So “nothing arrived” measured by looking at the Meshtastic app on the far node tells you nothing. This test settles it properly.

It sends a traceroute (portnum 70, which every repeater relays) to map the path, then a direct packet on the Meshvox portnum with want_ack. The far node’s firmware answers that acknowledgement itself, so it proves physical delivery even with nothing running at that end. If that fails, it repeats the probe on the text portnum: if the text gets through and Meshvox does not, a repeater on the path is relaying only core portnums.

Why port 260 instead of a text message?

Meshtastic reserves portnums 256–511 for private applications. Stock firmware routes and rebroadcasts purely on the mesh header — it never inspects the portnum — so a private-port packet floods exactly like a text message would. Stock clients ignore it, so voice bursts never land in anyone's chat window. No firmware changes, no recompile.

Payload goes to your own node in the clear over USB/BLE; the node applies the channel AES key itself, so Meshvox traffic is encrypted exactly as well as everything else on that channel.

Codec reasoning — why 6 kbps Opus

LongFast carries about 1.07 kbps of raw chip rate, and after headers, pacing and flood duplication the usable goodput for one sender is closer to 20–60 bytes per second. Anything above ~8 kbps of audio makes a 10-second clip take longer to transmit than the sentence would take to type.

Opus at 6 kbps / 60 ms frames lands at ~45 bytes per frame and stays intelligible for speech. It is encoded in-browser by WebCodecs, so nothing on the radio needs to know what a codec is. DTX replaces silent frames with a 1-byte marker, which typically removes 20–40% of a real voice message.

How the scheduler stays inside the airtime limits

A file is not a voice clip. Sending 80 kB over LongFast is hours of transmission, and a naive sender would either melt the channel or get its packets silently dropped by its own firmware. The scheduler enforces two independent sliding-window budgets before every single packet, and waits out whichever one binds:

1. Regulatory duty cycle. A rolling 60-minute ledger of actual time-on-air. EU 433/868 allow 10%, i.e. 360 s per hour. Before each packet the scheduler checks whether sending it would breach the budget, and if so computes exactly when enough old airtime ages out of the window, then sleeps until then. Not an average, not a guess — the same rolling window the regulation describes.

2. Channel utilisation. A rolling 60-second ledger of channel occupancy including every relay that will rebroadcast you. Meshtastic firmware refuses to transmit non-critical traffic above 25% channel utilisation, so exceeding it does not just annoy your neighbours, it stalls your own queue.

On top of both there is a minimum inter-packet gap, so a transfer paces smoothly instead of bursting until it hits a wall. The result: a transfer that would take four hours under EU duty-cycle rules simply takes four hours, trickling at exactly the permitted rate, and the estimate says so before you start. Everything is resumable — pause releases the channel without losing progress.

Blocks are coded and repaired independently, so a long transfer delivers incrementally and a receiver only ever asks for repair on the blocks it is actually short on.

What actually happens when packets are lost

Losses are erasures, not corruption. The LoRa PHY CRC drops damaged frames before Meshtastic ever sees them, and every Meshvox packet carries its own CRC-16 as well. A receiver therefore always knows exactly which numbered pieces are missing — the precondition for erasure coding to be worth anything.

Systematic Reed–Solomon over GF(256). The clip becomes k source packets plus r repair packets. Any k of the k+r reconstruct it exactly; which ones arrive is irrelevant. Lose packets 2, 7 and 19, receive three repairs, get a bit-exact clip.

Repair is universal, so one packet fixes everybody. On a broadcast mesh every listener lost a different subset. Selective retransmission would have to re-send the union of everyone's losses. With RS, one fresh repair packet advances every receiver by one regardless of what each is missing. That is the main reason not to use plain ARQ here.

Repair requests are O(1). A receiver never enumerates what it lost — it says only "I need 4 more", in a 10-byte packet. Requests are suppressed: a node cancels its own if it overhears one asking for at least as many, and the neediest node backs off least, so a single request usually covers the whole mesh.

If it still fails you get degraded audio, not silence. Opus frames are packed into one continuous stream and sliced at packet boundaries, and each packet carries a one-byte pointer to the first frame header that starts inside it. So a lone surviving packet is self-synchronising — it can be parsed and played on its own, losing at most the split frame at each end. A message that never reaches k still plays as speech with gaps where the missing packets were, and the trick costs one byte per packet instead of the ~18% that hard frame-alignment would waste.
